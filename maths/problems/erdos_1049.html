Erdős problem #1049

Lambert-type series at rational bases

For which rational bases is the corresponding series irrational? The first resistant explicit base is three halves.

The problem

The question

Choose a rational number 𝑡 >1t>1. Form its powers 𝑡,𝑡2,𝑡3,…t,t2,t3,…, subtract 1 from each, and add their reciprocals: 𝐹(𝑡) =1/(𝑡 −1) +1/(𝑡2 −1) +1/(𝑡3 −1) +⋯F(t)=1/(t−1)+1/(t2−1)+1/(t3−1)+⋯.

The terms shrink fast enough to give a finite total. Chowla’s question, recorded as Erdős #1049, asks whether that total can ever be a fraction of two integers.

An example

At 𝑡 =2t=2, the sum is 1 +1/3 +1/7 +1/15 +⋯1+1/3+1/7+1/15+⋯; Erdős proved its total irrational. At 𝑡 =3/2t=3/2, it starts 2 +4/5 +8/19 +⋯2+4/5+8/19+⋯: for example, (3/2)2 −1 =5/4(3/2)2−1=5/4, whose reciprocal is 4/54/5.

Whether the total at 3/2 is irrational remains unknown. Every finite sum is rational; the question concerns the limit of all the terms.

Let 𝑡 >1t>1 be a rational number. Is ∑∞𝑛=11𝑡𝑛−1 =∑∞𝑛=1𝜏(𝑛)𝑡𝑛∑n=1∞1tn−1=∑n=1∞τ(n)tn irrational, where 𝜏(𝑛)τ(n) counts the divisors of 𝑛n?

Erdős problem #1049, as erdosproblems.com/1049 states it

The setup in detail

Write the rational base in lowest terms as 𝑡 =𝑎/𝑏t=a/b, with integers 𝑎 >𝑏 ≥1a>b≥1. Integer bases are included by taking 𝑏 =1b=1. For each positive integer 𝑛n, the term 1/(𝑡𝑛 −1)1/(tn−1) is a positive rational number. The sum is finite because

𝑡𝑛−1=(𝑡−1)(𝑡𝑛−1+⋯+1)≥(𝑡−1)𝑡𝑛−1,tn−1=(t−1)(tn−1+⋯+1)≥(t−1)tn−1,

so its terms are bounded by a geometric series with ratio 1/𝑡 <11/t<1. A limit of fractions need not be a fraction: finite partial sums alone cannot settle the question.

Irrational means unequal to every ratio 𝑟/𝑠r/s of integers with 𝑠 >0s>0. A useful way to prove this is to construct positive numbers 𝑄𝑁𝐹(𝑡) −𝑃𝑁QNF(t)−PN with integer coefficients that tend to zero. If 𝐹(𝑡) =𝑟/𝑠F(t)=r/s, each such number equals (𝑄𝑁𝑟 −𝑃𝑁𝑠)/𝑠(QNr−PNs)/s; being positive, it must be at least 1/𝑠1/s. It cannot tend to zero.

Erdős proved irrationality for every integer base at least 2 in 1948. Some noninteger rational bases are also settled. The result panel explains a sufficient condition covering 31/4 and all its positive integer powers. It does not cover 3/2, and the question for every rational base remains open.

The example in detail

Why do divisor counts occur in the graph’s version of the sum? At base 2, expand each reciprocal as a geometric series:

12𝑛−1=12𝑛+122𝑛+123𝑛+⋯.12n−1=12n+122n+123n+⋯.

Row 𝑛n contributes at exponents that are multiples of 𝑛n. For instance, 1/261/26 appears in rows 1, 2, 3 and 6, because those are exactly the divisors of 6. Its coefficient is therefore 𝜏(6) =4τ(6)=4. Collecting equal powers gives

1+13+17+115+⋯=12+222+223+324+225+426+⋯.1+13+17+115+⋯=12+222+223+324+225+426+⋯.

The six terms on the right sum to 100/64 =1.5625100/64=1.5625; their remaining tail brings the total to 1.60669…1.60669…. The displayed terms on the left and right are not equal finite sums: the equality is between the two infinite totals. All terms are positive, which justifies regrouping them.

The same expansion works with any 𝑡 >1t>1 in place of 2. Thus 𝐹(𝑡) =∑𝑛≥1𝜏(𝑛)/𝑡𝑛F(t)=∑n≥1τ(n)/tn. This form has predictable denominators and is the starting point for the graph’s clearing calculation.

The crux

The graph tests one approach. Rewrite the total as 𝐹(𝑡) =∑𝑛≥1𝜏(𝑛)/𝑡𝑛F(t)=∑n≥1τ(n)/tn, where 𝜏(𝑛)τ(n) counts divisors: 𝜏(6) =4τ(6)=4 from 1, 2, 3 and 6. Write 𝑡 =𝑎/𝑏t=a/b with integers 𝑎 >𝑏 ≥1a>b≥1, and multiply by 𝑎𝑁aN. Each of the first 𝑁N products is an integer; the graph plots the positive leftover 𝐸𝑁EN after removing them.

If 𝐹(𝑡) =𝑝/𝑞F(t)=p/q were a fraction, every positive leftover would be at least 1/𝑞1/q; leftovers tending to zero would contradict this. Blue uses base 2; red uses 3/2. Each vertical step is a factor of 10. The red curve grows above the dashed bound 2𝑁+12N+1. This approach fails; the result below finds different combinations that do tend to zero at suitable bases.

For 𝑡 =𝑎/𝑏t=a/b, the divisor-count form is 𝐹(𝑡) =∑𝑛≥1𝜏(𝑛)𝑏𝑛/𝑎𝑛F(t)=∑n≥1τ(n)bn/an. Multiplying by 𝑎𝑁aN makes its first N terms integers. Remove those terms and call what remains 𝐸𝑁EN:

𝑎𝑁𝐹(𝑎/𝑏)=𝑁∑𝑛=1𝜏(𝑛)𝑏𝑛𝑎𝑁−𝑛⎵⎵⎵⎵⎵⎵⎵⎵integer+𝐸𝑁,𝐸𝑁=𝑏𝑁∑𝑚≥1𝜏(𝑁+𝑚)(𝑏/𝑎)𝑚.aNF(a/b)=∑n=1Nτ(n)bnaN−n⏟integer+EN,EN=bN∑m≥1τ(N+m)(b/a)m.

The graph follows 𝐸𝑁EN as 𝑁N increases from 0 to 20. It is a multiplied tail, not the original sum’s tail. If 𝐹(𝑡) =𝑟/𝑠F(t)=r/s, then 𝑠𝐸𝑁sEN is a positive integer. Making 𝐸𝑁EN arbitrarily small would therefore contradict rationality.

At base 2, the blue points stay between 1 and 5 in the displayed range. For example, 𝐸1 =2𝐹(2) −1 =2.21339…E1=2F(2)−1=2.21339…. These points alone provide no contradiction. Erdős’s integer-base proof instead uses divisibility of consecutive divisor counts to force special patterns in the base expansion; it needs more than this simple tail.

At base 3/23/2, the clearing multiplier is 3𝑁3N. The factor 2𝑁2N survives in the tail formula. Since every divisor count is at least 1,

𝐸𝑁≥2𝑁∑𝑚≥1(2/3)𝑚=2𝑁+1.EN≥2N∑m≥1(2/3)m=2N+1.

This is the red dashed lower bound. At 𝑁 =10N=10 it is 2048, while 𝐸10 =7165.1…E10=7165.1…; by 𝑁 =20N=20 the red points exceed 8.5 million. Each equal vertical step in the graph is a factor of 10. Thus clearing this straightforward expansion makes the leftover larger, rather than giving the small positive expression an irrationality proof would need.

Zudilin’s 2004 construction uses carefully chosen polynomial combinations of the same total. Their small positive remainders compete with the cost of making the coefficients integers. Cancelling common polynomial factors first lowers that cost. The result panel follows this operation at 31/4, where the remainders do tend to zero.

For coprime 𝑎 >𝑏 ≥1a>b≥1, the paper proves irrationality when log⁡𝑏/log⁡𝑎 <𝜃∗ =0.4056830213840605…log⁡b/log⁡a<θ∗=0.4056830213840605…. The simpler strict cutoff 81/200 =0.40581/200=0.405 is rigorously below 𝜃∗θ∗; 4200 <31814200<3181 places 31/4 inside it. A common positive power preserves the logarithmic ratio. The forms and constants are Zudilin’s; the paper supplies the rational-base degree calculation. Zudilin previously noted rational-base extensions and regards this consequence as hard to classify as a new result. The base 3/2 and the universal question remain open.

Results and open questions

#1049. For positive integers 0 <𝑏 <𝑎0<b<a in the exact Zudilin contour region, Lean checks irrationality and the stated irrationality-exponent bound for 𝐹(𝑎/𝑏)F(a/b), including every positive integral power of 31/431/4. The short paper also gives an ordinary proof for coprime 𝑎,𝑏a,b under log⁡𝑏/log⁡𝑎 <𝜃∗log⁡b/log⁡a<θ∗, where 𝜃∗ ≈0.4056830214θ∗≈0.4056830214, using Zudilin’s 2004 forms and estimates. Zudilin’s prior work is credited; formalisation does not establish novelty. Lean also checks the normalized Hankel determinant’s order and leading coefficient at every rank.

Separately, for each real 𝑝 >1p>1 and 1 ≤𝑁 ≤81≤N≤8, the Lean-checked finite coefficient pencil has a positive definite first matrix, real roots strictly below 𝐹(𝑝)F(p), and non-strict interlacing at adjacent ranks. The eight unshifted determinant certificates are kernel checked; the shifted eight and 76 cyclotomic residue witnesses remain finite computations. These results give no all-rank coefficient positivity or irrationality at 3/23/2, which lies outside the contour region. The universal rational-base question remains open. Statements with Lean proofs

Lean is software that verifies a formal proof against its theorem statement and assumptions. These proofs establish only the statements listed here.

  • Lean checks irrationality and the stated irrationality-exponent bound for rational bases in the exact Zudilin contour region, including every positive integral power of 31/431/4. The contour excludes 3/23/2.
  • For p>1 and 𝑁 ≤8N≤8, Lean checks positive definite coefficient matrices, real finite-pencil roots below F(p), and non-strict interlacing at adjacent ranks. Neither all-rank positivity nor irrationality at 3/23/2 follows.
  • That a literal coordinatewise transfer of the integer-base clearing argument forces a power-versus-linear inequality, and that this inequality is impossible at base three halves.
  • The exact denominator-cleared rational-base tail recurrence, including its forcing term.
  • That for every noninteger base with denominator at least two the forcing magnitude grows at least exponentially, while at denominator one it collapses to a bounded expression.
  • The complete elementary Archimedean height inequality used by the published Bundschuh-Väänänen criterion at base seven halves; the analytic irrationality theorem itself remains external.
  • Polynomial bounds on the doubled denominator exponents in the homogenised Padé construction, together with an exact gap identity.
  • The exact exterior-determinant elimination identity, and that rowwise contents scale the Padé errors, exterior determinant, and absolute determinant height by the same factors.
  • The exact Heine-Zudilin cone endpoint exponent arithmetic used to identify the bottom and top coefficient endpoints.
  • That homogeneous evaluation at (3,2) sees only the constant endpoint modulo three and the top endpoint modulo two, so unit endpoints obstruct common local factors.
  • That under unit top and constant endpoint hypotheses, a common multiplier of the two specialised coefficient channels is divisible by neither two nor three; the missing local gain must therefore survive primitive normalisation.
  • That vanishing of the higher bottom and top endpoint jets is exactly divisibility by the corresponding powers of three and two.
  • That the source integer-base ratio inequality implies a strictly negative scalar product-formula margin at three halves.
  • That the four-jet target has exact cardinality (3𝑅)2(2𝑆)2(3R)2(2S)2 and, for positive R, any family with n at least 4R+2S forces two distinct binary selectors to collide; their {-1,0,1} difference cancels all four endpoint jets.
  • That the rectangular Hermite-Pade threshold never improves on the classical one in the checked exponent model, with equality characterised exactly.
  • The exact 81/20081/200 height region: 31/431/4 and every positive power lie inside it and outside the earlier Bundschuh-Vaananen region, while 3/23/2 lies outside both.
