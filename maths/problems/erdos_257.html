Reciprocal sums over infinite exponent supports

Is the sum of 1/(2𝑛 −1)1/(2n−1) over every infinite set of positive exponents irrational?

The problem

The question

The powers of 2, minus 1, are 1,3,7,15,31,…1,3,7,15,31,…. Take their reciprocals and choose infinitely many, without repetition. Write 𝐴A for the chosen positive exponents and 𝑋 =∑𝑛∈𝐴1/(2𝑛 −1)X=∑n∈A1/(2n−1) for the sum. Every choice converges. Must its value always be irrational, meaning unequal to every ratio of integers?

An example

The diagram uses exponents made by multiplying 2s and 3s: 𝐴 ={1,2,3,4,6,8,9,12,…}A={1,2,3,4,6,8,9,12,…}. Its sum begins 𝑋 =1 +1/3 +1/7 +1/15 +1/63 +⋯X=1+1/3+1/7+1/15+1/63+⋯. This case is known to be irrational: the criterion ∑𝑛∈𝐴1/𝑛 <∞∑n∈A1/n<∞ applies, with reciprocal exponent sum 3. The unrestricted problem imposes no such condition.

Let 𝐴 ⊆ℕA⊆N be an infinite set. Is ∑𝑛∈𝐴12𝑛−1∑n∈A12n−1 irrational?

Erdős problem #257, as erdosproblems.com/257 states it

The setup in detail

The exponent 𝑛n starts at 1: exponent 0 would give a zero denominator. For every positive 𝑛n, 2𝑛 −1 ≥2𝑛−12n−1≥2n−1, so

0<𝑋≤∑𝑛≥121−𝑛=2.0<X≤∑n≥121−n=2.

Thus convergence is automatic, even before a choice of exponents is made. The issue is rationality of the limit. Finite selections always have rational sums; for example, 1/3 +1/7 =10/211/3+1/7=10/21. Rational partial sums can nevertheless have an irrational limit, so checking finitely many terms cannot decide the question.

All denominators 2𝑛 −12n−1 are odd. Consequently, every finite selection has an odd denominator when reduced to lowest terms. This restriction need not survive an infinite limit: whether an infinite selection sums to 1/21/2 is itself still unknown.

The example in detail

The displayed exponents are exactly 𝐴 ={2𝑖3𝑗 :𝑖,𝑗 ≥0}A={2i3j:i,j≥0}. Their reciprocal exponent sum is

∑𝑖,𝑗≥02−𝑖3−𝑗=(1+1/2+1/4+⋯)(1+1/3+1/9+⋯)=3.∑i,j≥02−i3−j=(1+1/2+1/4+⋯)(1+1/3+1/9+⋯)=3.

Erdős stated that finite reciprocal exponent sum suffices for irrationality; Appendix A of the short paper supplies a proof. This is a sufficient condition, not a condition required by the problem.

To read a row, the binary places from left to right have values 1/2,1/4,1/8,…1/2,1/4,1/8,…. A dot means that place is included. The geometric-series identity

12𝑛−1=2−𝑛+2−2𝑛+2−3𝑛+⋯12n−1=2−n+2−2n+2−3n+⋯

puts dots at every 𝑛nth place. Each row continues forever; only 24 places and the exponents at most 24 are drawn. The rows represent separate summands, rather than the binary digits of their total.

Multiplication by 212212 shifts the binary point 12 places. Subtracting the original row cancels its fractional part exactly when its period divides 12. For row 3, (212 −1)/7 =585(212−1)/7=585 is an integer. For row 8, (212 −1)/255 =16 +1/17(212−1)/255=16+1/17, so the remainder is 1/17 =0.0588…1/17=0.0588…, as printed. The final line includes all omitted rows.

The crux

Each row represents one reciprocal in binary: row 3 has a dot at every third binary place, since 1/7 =1/8 +1/64 +1/512 +⋯1/7=1/8+1/64+1/512+⋯. The cut is after 12 places. Amber rows repeat across it; multiplying their values by 212 −1212−1 gives integers. The other rows leave the fractional remainders listed at right. Their total is positive. If 𝑋 =𝑝/𝑞X=p/q, that total must be at least 1/𝑞1/q. Choosing cut positions with arbitrarily small positive totals therefore proves irrationality. The displayed cut illustrates this test; it does not settle every infinite choice.

For any positive integer 𝑁N, divide it by an exponent 𝑛n: write 𝑁 =𝑢𝑛 +𝑟N=un+r with 0 ≤𝑟 <𝑛0≤r<n. Then

2𝑁−12𝑛−1=𝑢∑𝑗=12𝑁−𝑗𝑛+2𝑟−12𝑛−1.2N−12n−1=∑j=1u2N−jn+2r−12n−1.

The first sum is an integer and the final fraction lies between 0 and 1. It vanishes exactly when 𝑛n divides 𝑁N. Adding these fractions gives

Δ(𝑁)=∑𝑛∈𝐴2𝑁mod𝑛−12𝑛−1=(2𝑁−1)𝑋−𝐽(𝑁),𝐽(𝑁)∈ℤ.Δ(N)=∑n∈A2Nmodn−12n−1=(2N−1)X−J(N),J(N)∈Z.

Here 𝑁mod𝑛Nmodn denotes the remainder on division by 𝑛n, and 𝐽(𝑁)J(N) is the sum of the integer parts removed. Only finitely many are nonzero, since they vanish for 𝑛 >𝑁n>N. The total Δ(𝑁)Δ(N) is a sum of fractional parts; it can exceed 1.

If 𝑋 =𝑝/𝑞X=p/q with integers 𝑝p and 𝑞 >0q>0, then 𝑞Δ(𝑁)qΔ(N) is an integer. It is positive because an infinite 𝐴A contains an exponent larger than 𝑁N, whose remainder is positive. Hence Δ(𝑁) ≥1/𝑞Δ(N)≥1/q. This explains why arbitrarily small positive totals contradict rationality.

For the pictured set, choosing 𝑁 =6𝑚N=6m cancels every row 2𝑖3𝑗2i3j with 𝑖,𝑗 ≤𝑚i,j≤m. The first four totals are 0.586…,0.0595…,0.00779…,0.0000152…0.586…,0.0595…,0.00779…,0.0000152…. These values illustrate the mechanism; the reciprocal-summability theorem justifies its use for the infinite set. At the displayed cut 𝑁 =12N=12, the total is 0.1509…0.1509….

This particular test has limits. For all positive exponents, the terms above 𝑁N alone give Δ(𝑁) >1 −2−𝑁 ≥1/2Δ(N)>1−2−N≥1/2; for prime exponents the short paper proves Δ(𝑁) >1/3Δ(N)>1/3. Both sums are known to be irrational by other arguments. The weighted theorem explained in the result panel makes small totals for further classes of exponents, but arbitrary infinite choices remain open.

Results and open questions

#257. Fix a finite nonempty set of primes 𝑃P and let ℎ(𝑎)h(a) be the largest divisor of 𝑎a supported on 𝑃P. For an infinite support 𝐴A and an integer base 𝑏 ≥2b≥2, finiteness of ∑𝑎∈𝐴ℎ(𝑎)/(𝑎(𝑏ℎ(𝑎) −1))∑a∈Ah(a)/(a(bh(a)−1)) implies irrationality of ∑𝑎∈𝐴1/(𝑏𝑎 −1)∑a∈A1/(ba−1). The long paper explains the proof: if this sum were rational with denominator 𝑣v, every positive displacement from an integer would be at least 1/𝑣1/v. Choose a finite part of 𝐴A and make its exponents divide an observation modulus 𝑄Q, so their displacements vanish.

For the remaining exponents, a complete residue orbit gives the weighted main term. Averaging over a finite block of dyadic observation lengths charges the incomplete orbits by weighted reciprocal mass, uniformly over finite subfamilies; this uniform bound permits the passage to the infinite support. The short paper then chooses the modulus and block length so that its error charged to the whole weighted mass tends to zero. The Lean proof first makes the remaining tail weight small and uses a different block-length schedule. Both produce a positive displacement below 1/𝑣1/v; their parameter schedules should be read with their respective error bounds.

Lean proves this as divisibilityWeightedClaim in WeightedReturn.lean. The short paper gives a shorter proof and an explicit support with divergent reciprocal sum satisfying the criterion. The comparison is with Erdős’s earlier reciprocal-summable condition, which he stated for every integer base after proving the pairwise-coprime case. For 𝑃 ={2}P={2}, write 𝑎 =2𝑘𝑚a=2km with 𝑚m odd: the weighted summand is 1/[𝑚(𝑏2𝑘 −1)]1/[m(b2k−1)]. In the short paper’s example 𝐴⋆ ={2𝑘𝑚 :𝑘 ≥1,𝑚 odd ,𝑚 ≤22𝑘}A⋆={2km:k≥1,m odd ,m≤22k}, dyadic blocks show that the odd reciprocals in the 𝑘kth layer sum to between 2𝑘/42k/4 and 2𝑘2k.

The layer therefore contributes at least 1/41/4 to ∑𝑎∈𝐴⋆1/𝑎∑a∈A⋆1/a, but at most 21−𝑘21−k to the base-two weighted sum. The ordinary reciprocal sum diverges while the weighted sum converges. The theorem gives irrationality at every integer base for every infinite subset of 𝐴⋆A⋆. It does not cover full support, all odd exponents, or the full prime support; Tao–Teräväinen prove the latter at base two by another method. The #257 reader exercise tests how changing a layer cutoff alters this certificate, and why a failed weighted test is not a rationality result.

A second test shows why a prime set fitted separately to each finite prefix cannot certify the infinite hypothesis. The weighted-support transfer exercise gives the complementary fixed-host phenomenon: for any prescribed finite nonempty prime set, its binary weighted certificate can require every prime in that set. This is a calculation about the sufficient criterion, not a claim that each infinite subset needs the same witness or that the example has been formalised separately in Lean. The comparison identifies the added class and proof mechanism, but does not settle independent novelty or priority assessment.

See the short paper’s theorem, example and sources. One host realises any finite monotone witness rule, checked in Lean as finite_monotone_witness_rule_realised. Given a finite set EE of primes and a nonconstant upward-closed rule on its subsets, a finite union of prime-cofactor blocks has divergent reciprocal sum, its weighted test converges at every base exactly when the witness primes in EE satisfy the rule, and every infinite subset has irrational subseries at every integer base. For three primes, any two suffice and none is individually mandatory. The worked variants compare this host with a different required-prime construction.

The construction is AI-assisted and its novelty has not been independently assessed. At base two, the weighted condition can also be combined with the positive divisor-cover criterion: the long paper proves that a common finite averaging window makes both displacements small. Every infinite subset of their union then has irrational subseries at every integer base. Lean proves this as mixedSupportClaim in the same module. Neither theorem establishes the separately proposed hosts separating the two classes. Every infinite reciprocal-summable support satisfies it, yielding the coprimality-free extension stated by Erdős.

Full-support irrationality at every integer base is classical (Erdős 1948) and Lean-checked here, as are pairwise-coprime summable-reciprocal support and Lebesgue measure one for the base-2 achievement set. Irrationality for every infinite support and the 1/21/2 and 1/211/21 branches remain open. The weighted theorem is Lean-checked. Statements with Lean proofs

Lean is software that verifies a formal proof against its theorem statement and assumptions. These proofs establish only the statements listed here.

  • Irrationality at every integer base for the classical full support, reciprocal-summable supports, and the finite-prime weighted supports in divisibilityWeightedClaim.
  • Every infinite subset of the union of a finite base-two weighted-mass host and a strengthened positive-cover host has irrational series at every integer base (mixedSupportClaim).
  • Unique Mersenne coding on every support; compactness and nowhere density; perfection for infinite support; finite-complement measure 2−|𝐹|2−|F|, otherwise zero.
What these proofs do not establish
  • Universal irrationality over all infinite supports, or the proposed rational values 1/21/2 and 1/211/21.
  • Fair-bit laws, finer singularity results, and periodic-stride dimensions.
  • Any bridge from geometric uniqueness or measure rigidity to arithmetic irrationality.
