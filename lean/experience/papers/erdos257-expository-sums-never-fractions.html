The question: which infinite sums are irrational?

Every finite sum of fractions is a fraction, and an infinite sum can be one too: 12+14+18+…=1.\frac12+\frac14+\frac18+\dots=1. Now consider S=13+115+1255+165535+…,S=\frac13+\frac1{15}+\frac1{255}+\frac1{65535}+\dots, where the denominators are 22−1,24−1,28−1,216−1,…2^2-1,2^4-1,2^8-1,2^{16}-1,\dots and the exponents double at each step. We will prove that this sum is equal to no fraction at all.

To see where such a proof might come from, multiply by 255255, the third denominator: 255S=85+17+1+25565535+…=103+1257+….\begin{aligned} 255S&=85+17+1+\frac{255}{65535}+\dots\\ &=103+\frac1{257}+\dots. \end{aligned} The first three terms have become the integer 103103, and a positive remainder is left over. If S=p/qS=p/q with integers p,qp,q and q>0q>0, then multiplying by an integer and subtracting an integer leaves an integer multiple of 1/q1/q, so a positive remainder must be at least 1/q1/q. Our plan is to repeat this construction until the whole remainder is smaller than that fixed gap, whatever denominator qq is proposed.

The example belongs to a larger family, whose fractions are obtained by taking a power of 22, subtracting 11, and taking the reciprocal:

A basic property of the real numbers says that an increasing bounded sequence has a finite limit (Appendix A.1), and the question is whether this limit is rational.

Erdős’s question and the theorem

In a 1988 account, Erdős wrote [3] (excerpt):

“let n1<n2<…n_1<n_2<\dots. Is it then true that ∑12nk−1\sum \frac{1}{2^{n_k}-1} is irrational, …”

Here the indices k=1,2,…k=1,2,\dots count the chosen exponents nkn_k. In these terms, we shall prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1. Let n1<n2<…n_1<n_2<\dots be an infinite sequence of positive integers. If ∑k=1∞1/nk<∞\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}1/n_k<\infty, then ∑k=1∞12nk−1\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac1{2^{n_k}-1} is irrational.

With A={n1,n2,…}A=\{n_1,n_2,\dots\} this series is SAS_A, and the hypothesis says that ∑n∈A1/n<∞\sum_{n\in A}1/n<\infty. Erdős’s question for arbitrary infinite AA remains open [4].

The hypothesis concerns the reciprocals of the exponents, while the conclusion concerns the fractions with denominators 2n−12^n-1. For the doubling exponents one table shows the difference:

The second row sums to 11, so the theorem says that the third row sums to an irrational number. Every SAS_A converges, so the hypothesis restricts AA further, for the sake of the irrationality proof.

We shall use the hypothesis in the following form: beyond a sufficiently large exponent, the remaining reciprocals 1/n1/n add up to less than any positive target we specify. Such a remaining sum is called a tail.

The square exponents A={1,4,9,16,…}A=\{1,4,9,16,\dots\} also satisfy the hypothesis. Indeed, for m≥2m\ge2, 1m2<1m(m−1)=1m−1−1m,\frac1{m^2}<\frac1{m(m-1)}=\frac1{m-1}-\frac1m, so the comparison sum cancels: (1−12)+(12−13)+(13−14)+…+(1N−1−1N)=1−1N<1.(1-\tfrac12)+(\tfrac12-\tfrac13)+(\tfrac13-\tfrac14) +\dots+(\tfrac1{N-1}-\tfrac1N)=1-\tfrac1N<1. Thus ∑m=2N1/m2<1\sum_{m=2}^{N}1/m^2<1, so ∑m=1∞1/m2\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}1/m^2 converges, and the theorem proves that 1+115+1511+165535+…1+\tfrac1{15}+\tfrac1{511}+\tfrac1{65535}+\dots is irrational.

Choosing every positive integer, on the other hand, puts the divergent harmonic series into the hypothesis. To see the divergence, group its terms as 11; 1/21/2; 1/3+1/41/3+1/4; 1/5+1/6+1/7+1/81/5+1/6+1/7+1/8; and so on. For example, 15+16+17+18≥4⋅18=12.\frac15+\frac16+\frac17+\frac18\ge4\cdot\frac18=\frac12. Each later block doubles the number of terms and halves their smallest value, so it too contributes at least 1/21/2, and these repeated contributions make the partial sums unbounded. The even exponents fail the hypothesis as well, since their reciprocal sum is half the harmonic series, and the theorem makes no assertion about either choice. Infinitude matters too: A={2,4}A=\{2,4\} has a finite reciprocal sum, but SA=13+115=25S_A=\tfrac13+\tfrac1{15}=\tfrac25 (Question 1).

Erdős proved the theorem in 1968 under the extra assumption that no two chosen exponents share a divisor greater than 11, and remarked that this assumption could be removed, without giving the details [2]. We explain a proof that does without it.

The proof has three stages. First, rationality forces a fixed lower bound on certain positive remainders. Next, the special form of the denominators 2n−12^n-1 lets us calculate those remainders. Finally, an average guarantees at least one choice whose total remainder lies below that lower bound. The doubling exponents show the construction at work before any averaging is needed, and the main proof ends in Section 7.2. Appendices A.1–A.4 justify the supporting facts about infinite sums and divisibility, and can be read as needed or after the main proof; the extension to other bases and the comparison with the formal proof are further reading.

A rational number has a fixed positive gap

Start with 2/52/5. Multiplying it by an integer and subtracting an integer always gives an integer multiple of 1/51/5: 3⋅25−1=15,4⋅25−1=35,5⋅25−2=0,1⋅25−1=−35.3\cdot\frac25-1=\frac15,\quad 4\cdot\frac25-1=\frac35,\quad 5\cdot\frac25-2=0,\quad 1\cdot\frac25-1=-\frac35. No such value lies strictly between 00 and 1/51/5, because the denominator fixes a smallest possible positive result.

The same reasoning applies to any fraction. From now on we write S=SAS=S_A for the series belonging to our chosen set AA and suppose that S=p/qS=p/q, where p∈ℤp\in\mathbb Z and qq is a positive integer. For integers u,ju,j, uS−j=up−jqq.uS-j=\frac{up-jq}{q}. The numerator is an integer, so if uS−j>0uS-j>0, then up−jq≥1up-jq\ge1 and uS−j≥1q.uS-j\ge\frac1q. Positivity matters here, since zero and negative values are perfectly compatible with rationality.

To prove our sum irrational, we shall therefore suppose that S=p/qS=p/q and construct integers u,ju,j, which may depend on qq, such that 0<uS−j<1q.\begin{equation} \label{eq:goal} 0<uS-j<\frac1q. \end{equation}

What made this work is that each kk both eliminated all earlier terms and left the next exponent at least 2k2k. A general set AA need not have that spacing, and the next section explains how averaging takes its place.

Why averaging works

Larger multipliers need not help.

For the square exponents A={1,4,9,16,…}A=\{1,4,9,16,\dots\}, both k=4k=4 and k=8k=8 are divisible by the first two selected exponents 11 and 44, and all later selected exponents exceed 88, so their integer parts vanish for both multipliers. Average complete attempts.

Different rows may be small in different columns, and choosing a separate column for each row would give no single multiplier for the original series. Instead we average every row across the same columns and then add the row averages. The result is the average of the complete column totals, since both calculations add the same entries and divide by the same number MM. In symbols, 1M∑m=1MR(mQ)=∑n∈A1M∑m=1Mℓn(mQ).\begin{equation} \label{eq:average-main} \frac1M\sum_{m=1}^{M}R(mQ) =\sum_{n\in A}\frac1M\sum_{m=1}^{M}\ell_n(mQ). \end{equation}

With infinitely many rows we first use finite portions and then let them grow, and convergence preserves the equality (Appendix A.4). If this average is below 1/q1/q, then at least one whole column has total below 1/q1/q, for otherwise every column total would be at least 1/q1/q and so would their average.

What the two estimates will do.

We shall divide the rows into three groups. An initial finite group stays zero. A further finite group can complete its cycles if MM is chosen suitably, and we shall bound the average of each such row by 1/n1/n. For the infinitely many remaining rows we need a bound valid for every MM, and we shall prove the bound 2Q/n2Q/n. Once QQ is fixed, the sum of these bounds becomes as small as we like if we move far enough down the rows, because the remaining reciprocals 1/n1/n have arbitrarily small total. Section 7 chooses the two row cutoffs, and then MM, so that both nonzero groups have small total averages.

Consequences and limits

For every integer b≥2b\ge2, the same method proves A infinite,∑n∈A1n<∞⇒∑n∈A1bn−1 is irrational.A\text{ infinite},\quad\sum_{n\in A}\frac1n<\infty \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sum_{n\in A}\frac1{b^n-1}\text{ is irrational}. We replace the multiplier by bk−1b^k-1; the fractional parts become (bkmod⁡n−1)/(bn−1)(b^{k\operatorname{mod} n}-1)/(b^n-1), their cycle averages are still below 1/n1/n, and Appendix A.5 checks both estimates.

The theorem covers every selection from the square-exponent series 1+115+1511+…1+\tfrac1{15}+\tfrac1{511}+\dots. If infinitely many terms remain, their sum is irrational, because deleting terms cannot increase the reciprocal sum ∑m≥11/m2<∞\sum_{m\ge1}1/m^2<\infty of the exponents, so Theorem 1 still applies; if only finitely many remain, their sum is rational. Thus a selection from this series has a rational sum if and only if it is finite, whatever pattern the retained exponents follow, and the same holds with 22 replaced by any integer b≥2b\ge2.

The reciprocal-sum condition is sufficient for irrationality, and the method stops short of necessity: the remainders R(k)R(k) need not become arbitrarily small for every infinite AA. When every positive exponent is selected, the terms with n>kn>k alone give R(k)≥∑n>k2k−12n−1>∑n>k2k−12n=1−2−k≥12.R(k)\ge\sum_{n>k}\frac{2^k-1}{2^n-1} >\sum_{n>k}\frac{2^k-1}{2^n} =1-2^{-k}\ge\frac12. These remainders therefore never fall below 1/21/2. Nevertheless, Erdős proved in 1948 that the full sum is irrational [1], and the failure of our method says nothing about rationality. Tao and Teräväinen proved irrationality for the prime exponents [5], another case with divergent reciprocal sum. The short paper [6] gives further sufficient conditions, and the long record [7] develops their proofs and the obstructions to extending them. Erdős’s question for every infinite set AA remains open.

Convergence and tails

For nonnegative terms ana_n, the partial sums ∑n=1Nan\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_n increase. Why the sampled remainders form a cycle

The elementary cancellation fact states that if positive integers c,uc,u are coprime and cc divides dudu, then cc divides dd. The example in Section 6.2 used c=3c=3 and u=2u=2, and for general coprime c,uc,u the same argument uses integers a,ba,b with au+bc=1au+bc=1. Adding the row averages

For finitely many rows n≤Hn\le H, adding the row averages is the same as averaging the column totals, since both operations add the same entries and divide by MM. Keep QQ and MM fixed. Given any positive target ε\varepsilon, each of the MM convergent column sums R(mQ)R(mQ) has tail less than ε\varepsilon beyond some row cutoff, and beyond the largest of these finitely many cutoffs all column tails, and therefore their average, are less than ε\varepsilon.
